A high strength maraging stainless steel (MSS) was developed utilizing multiple nanoprecipitates and reverted
austenite. After aging treatment, this steel shows remarkable mechanical properties with a yield strength of
~1750 MPa, a tensile strength of ~1910 MPa, and a total elongation of ~10.5 %. The precipitation and
strengthening mechanisms of multiple nanoprecipitates were clarified. The precipitation sequence upon aging
can be identified as: Ni-rich cluster→ Ni-rich cluster + Mo-rich cluster→ Ni3Ti + Mo-rich phase→ Ni3Ti + Morich phase + α′-Cr. Ni-rich cluster and Mo-rich cluster is the precursor of Ni3Ti and Mo-rich phase, respectively.
The Ni-rich clusters, Mo-rich clusters, Ni3Ti and α′-Cr, contribute to the precipitation strengthening mainly by
shearing mechanisms, among them the ordered strengthening plays the most significant role. Upon aging, Ni3Ti
is wrapped by Mo-rich phase, restraining their growth. In contrast, the Mo-rich phase can be significantly
coarsened, leading to a shift in the precipitation strengthening from dislocation shearing to Orowan looping
mechanism. The reverted austenite improves the ductility of the MSS via a transformation induced plasticity
(TRIP) effect.

1. Introduction
Maraging stainless steels (MSSs) with high strength, ductility and
corrosion resistance are the backbone of innovations in manufacturing,
energy, transportation, and safety, such as automotive engine housings,
aircraft landing gears, and cryogenic missiles [1]. These excellent
properties stem from the formation of a unique microstructure during
the aging treatment: hierarchically arranged lath martensite with a high
dislocation density and decorated by various nanoprecipitates and
reverted austenite (RA) that re-precipitates from martensite [2,3]. The
high dislocation density of the martensite matrix and the multiple
nanoprecipitates provide the high strength of MSSs [2,4]. The RA con­
tributes to the plasticity and toughness [5,6]. However, the complex
interactions between alloying elements and the varying microstructure
during aging tend to be difficult to achieve an excellent balance between
strength and ductility in MSSs. In particular, coarse nanoprecipitates or
severe grain boundary segregation of atoms can lead to brittle fracture

[7,8]. Therefore, it is essential to deeply investigate the precipitation
mechanism of nanoprecipitates during aging treatment.
Much attentions have been paid to the relationship between nano­
precipitates and mechanical properties, while the formation mechanism
of nanoprecipitates during aging treatment is not well understood
[9–11]. In MSSs, three types of nanoprecipitates can be formed during
aging treatment, including Mo-rich, Ni3Ti, and Cr-rich nanoprecipitates,
which significantly increased the strength [10]. The effect of alloying
elements on the precipitation precipitation-hardened steels was inves­
tigated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), atom probe to­
mography (APT) and first-principles calculations [12,13]. Tian et al.
found that the addition of Co can provide stronger thermodynamic
driving force to form Ni-Ti clusters in body-centered cubic (BCC) iron
and promote the formation of Ni3Ti [12]. The effect of multiple alloying
additions on the co-precipitation of Mo-rich, Ni3Ti and Cr-rich nano­
precipitates was investigated by Niu et al. [13]. They found that Mo
could enhance the chemical driving force and reduce the strain energy of

However, many studies usually neglect the investigation of the precip­
itation sequence mechanism during aging, which can significantly affect
the strength of the steels. The evolution mechanism of nanoprecipitates
in precipitation hardening steels through aging treatment process has an
important effect on the mechanical properties [12,14–16]. For example,
in maraging steels utilizing Cu and NiAl as nanoprecipitates, the ratio of
Ni and Al influenced the precipitation sequence during aging treatment,
resulting in significantly different mechanical properties [17,18].
Exceeding the aging peak, the precipitates begin to coarsen, and Cu-rich
precipitates with a 9R structure convert to face-centered cubic (FCC)
structures [19], and the NiAl-type precipitates gradually convert to
Ni2AlMn-type precipitates [20]. However, the precipitation sequence of
Mo-rich, Ni3Ti, and Cr-rich nanoprecipitates in maraging stainless steels
during aging is still not clear and requires further investigation to better
understand the strengthening mechanism.
Although precipitation strengthening can significantly increase the
strength of steels, it often sacrifices ductility to a certain extent [14]. The
phenomenon is a trade-off between strength and ductility [21–24]. To
overcome this issue, researchers have tried to introduce the trans­
formation induced plasticity (TRIP) effect of austenite to improve
ductility [5,25,26]. In maraging steels, the aging treatment is also
accompanied by RA re-precipitation [26,27]. Different insights are
available on the formation mechanism of RA. The segregation of
austenite stabilizing elements at grain boundaries, such as Ni and Mn,
can reduce the Gibbs free energy of austenite and promote the formation
of RA [28–30]. Heo et al. suggested that the RA was formed by the
coherent strain at the interface between Ni3Ti and the matrix as a
driving force through the climbing of the edge dislocations and the
diffusion of Fe atoms [31]. Typically, the RA in MSS can effectively
improve ductility [25,26]. However, how to introduce and better control
the formation of nanoprecipitates in RA remains unknown and needs to
be further explored.
In the present work, an ultra-high strength MSS was developed uti­
lizing multiple nanoprecipitates and RA. Microstructures at different
length scales were characterized by electron backscatter diffraction
(EBSD), TEM and APT. First principles calculations were also conducted
to explain the mechanism of nanoprecipitates formation. The objective
for this work is to examine the effect of aging on microstructural evo­
lution and mechanical properties in order to elucidate the mechanisms
of nanoprecipitation strengthening. Meanwhile, the mechanism of
nanoprecipitate formation during aging treatment is also elaborated.

2. Experimental methods
The MSS with a nominal composition of Fe-11.0Cr-4.0Co-8.0Ni0.5Ti-5.0Mo-0.1Si-0.002C (wt.%) was designed and fabricated. Alloy
rods, weighing approximately 500 g, were fabricated from constituent
elements (>99.95 at.% purity) by arc melting in an argon atmosphere on
a water-cooled copper vessel. Prior to alloy melting, the furnace cham­
ber was evacuated, flushed and backfilled with high purity argon. The
alloy rods were remelted at least five times to ensure the homogeneity
and dropped into a copper mold with dimensions of Φ 20 mm × 200
mm.
The as-cast ingots were hot-rolled at 1200 ◦ C from 20 to 2 mm in
thickness, followed by water quenching. The specimens were solid so­
lution treated at 1050 ◦ C for 1 h and quenched in ice-water mixture,
followed by cryogenic treatment in liquid nitrogen for 8 h in order to
obtain single-phase martensite (labeled as ST). The sheets were then
aged at 480 ◦ C for 15, 30 (labeled as SA30), 120, 300 (labeled as SA300),
3,000, 12,000 and 30,000 min (labeled as SA30000), respectively.
Hardness measurements were conducted using a Rockwell tester
with a load of 500 g for 15 s. At least ten indentations were measured to

APT was employed to gain further characterization for the evolution

The evolution of the nanoprecipitates were observed by APT at
different aging conditions, as shown Fig. 7(a–d). In the early stage of
aging, only Ni-rich clusters are present, while Mo-rich and α′-Cr phases
gradually appear with the increasing aging time. Fig. 7 (a1-d3) show the
change in the center composition of the nanoprecipitates and matrix at
different aging conditions. Ultimately, the Mo-rich and α′-Cr phase
coarsen, as shown in Fig. 7, which is consistent with the previous studies
[14,43,44]. It can be found that the concentrations of Ni and Ti in
Ni-rich cluster cores gradually increase, while the concentrations of Fe,
Co, Mo and Cr gradually decrease. Finally, the ratio of Ni to Ti in the
cluster cores approaches 3:1 and reaches a stable state, forming the
hexagonal structure Ni3Ti. Typically, MSS with a high dislocation den­
sity after solution treatment leads to the formation of Ni-rich cluster by
the diffusion of atoms along the dislocation lines during the early stages
of aging [33,45]. Fig. 7 (a) displays that Ni-rich cluster are precipitated
uniformly with a considerable number density. The matrix possessing
high dislocation density allows for the prevalence of continuous pre­
cipitation in the matrix. During plastic deformation, additional energy
from the anti-phase boundary is demanded by the dislocations shearing
these nanoprecipitates, which is the major factor for increasing the
strength at the early aging [45].

aging time increasing, some of the Mo-rich and α′-Cr phase form adja­

the exclusion of the core atoms of Ni-rich cluster also promotes the
heterogeneous nucleation of Mo-rich and α′-Cr phase. The nano­
precipitates possessing different shapes can be approximately trans­
formed into spheres of identical volume phases with an equivalent

4. Discussions
4.1. Precipitation mechanism
The APT results after solution treatment showed that Fe, Co, Ni, Ti,
Mo, Cr, and Si were homogeneously distributed without any enrichment
(Fig. 6). However, after aging at 480 ◦ C, Ni, Ti, Mo, Si and Cr atoms
become significantly enriched, forming Ni3Ti, Mo-rich and α′-Cr phases,
respectively (Fig. 6). The appearance of these nanoprecipitates follows a
certain precipitation sequence. Initially, Ni-rich clusters are formed. The
Mo-rich clusters appear with the increased aging time, and these Morich clusters prefer heterogeneous nucleation at interfaces between the
Ni-rich cluster and matrix. That is consistent with the findings for others
[10,45,48]. The reason is that Ni, Ti and Mo possess different diffusion
rates in α-Fe [49–52]. Meanwhile, the Ni-Ti precipitates cores expel Fe,
Co, Mo and Cr atoms and gradually form a stable Ni3Ti phase (Fig. 7).
With further prolonged aging time, the α′-Cr phase nucleates and grows.
The matrix shows the coexistence of Ni3Ti, Mo-rich and α′-Cr phases. It is
the precipitation sequence of the nanoprecipitates that leads to different
precipitation strengthening effects at different aging stages (Fig. 1).
Studies on the precipitation mechanism of MSS tend to focus on the
characteristics of the nanoprecipitates at peak aging, while ignoring the
sequence of the nanoprecipitates [53–55]. The nanoprecipitates at
different aging time plays a very important role in controlling the me­
chanical properties [55,56]. It is reported that the effect of Co on the
strength in maraging steels is extremely important [12]. The addition of
Co promotes the nucleation of nanoprecipitates and further increases the
number density of nanoprecipitates, thus enhancing the precipitation
hardening effect [42,57]. The results of APT show a random distribution
of Co atoms in the matrix (Fig. 6). Therefore, Fe-Co-based alloys with
randomly distributed Co were used in the first-principles calculation.

To verify the validity of the strengthening mechanism for various
precipitates, the concept of the critical radius was applied. The critical
radius is estimated when the strength increments produced by the
shearing and Orowan mechanisms are equal [13,71]. Beyond this crit­
ical radius, dislocations will bypass precipitates rather than shearing.
For Ni3Ti precipitates, the critical radius is 5.37 nm. The critical radius
of Mo-rich precipitates is calculated as 6.52 nm. The critical radius of
α′-Cr precipitate is 5.94 nm. The APT results indicate that the radius of
Ni3Ti precipitates is 0.95 nm, 1.11 nm, 3.43 nm and 3.89 nm after aging
for 30 min, 300 min, 3000 min and 30000 min, respectively, smaller
than the critical radius. Thus, it is reasonable that Ni3Ti precipitates
strengthens the steel by shearing mechanism. The radius of Mo-rich
clusters is 1.47 nm, which is smaller than the critical radius. So, the
shearing mechanism is reasonable. The size of Mo-rich phases developed
from Mo-rich clusters aging for 3000 min and 30000 min are 6.81 nm
and 8.02 nm respectively, which are larger than the critical radius. Thus,
the bypassing mechanism is reasonable. For α′-Cr precipitates, the size is
1.53 nm, which is smaller than the critical radius. So, the shearing
mechanism is reasonable.
