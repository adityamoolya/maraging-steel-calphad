A B S T R A C T

Handling Editor: P Rios

Developing ultra-high strength and high toughness maraging steel requires an effective route of a well-designed
heat treatment regimen to control the precipitates. In this study, we regulated the heterogeneous nucleation sites
through Mo-enriched particles to increase the nucleation rate of Ni3Ti. The Mo-enriched particles as Ni3Ti
precursors decrease the lattice mismatch between α-Fe and Ni3Ti, reducing crack formation at the interface. We
tailored the maraging steel utilizing duplex aging treatment (DAT) to break the conventional precipitation
pathway. The DAT comprises a solid solution treatment followed by a two-step aging treatment at 370 ◦ C and
480 ◦ C. The Mo-enriched particles precipitated after solid solution treatment due to the low solubility and
diffusion rate at low temperatures. The aging treatment conducted at 370 ◦ C enhances the density of the Moenriched particles and inhibits the formation of Ni3Ti. The pre-formed precipitates act as heterogeneous
nucleation sites for Ni3Ti during aging at 480 ◦ C, increasing the nucleation rate and reducing the size. As aging
continues, Mo segregates to the interface of Ni3Ti and the matrix, inhibiting the growth of Ni3Ti and forming
Fe7Mo2 precipitates. The DAT steel exhibits an excellent yield strength of 2093 MPa and fracture toughness of 77
MPa•m1/2 at room temperature. The well-regulated precipitates increase crack nucleation energy, forming a
curved primary crack and multiple secondary cracks, which reduce stress concentrations and increase fracture
toughness. The DAT strategy provides a new avenue for improving fracture toughness while maintaining high
strength in maraging steel.

1. Introduction
Maraging steel is a kind of unique ultra-high strength steel, which is
strengthened by intermetallic compound precipitations generated on
ultra-low carbon Fe–Ni martensite [1–3]. It has excellent mechanical
and processing properties and has been widely employed in aerospace,
machinery manufacturing, nuclear and military fields [4–6]. The typical
application of ultra-strong steel is low-pressure turbine shafts in
aero-engine, which are characterized by hollow, thin-walled, high
aspect-ratio and complex loads [7,8]. The low-pressure turbine shaft is
susceptible to bending deformation and stress concentration during
operation [9,10]. As a result, the prepared material must achieve
ultra-high strength and excellent fracture toughness [11,12]. However,
the fracture toughness of maraging steel is significantly reduced when

the yield strength exceeds 1.9 GPa, which severely limits its engineering
applications [13,14]. Therefore, it is imperative to develop maraging
steel that demonstrates greater strength and toughness.
The type and morphology of precipitates significantly affect the
strengthening mechanism and mechanical properties. Ultra-high
strength of maraging steel is achieved by the precipitation of finely
dispersed particles [2]. The most commonly formed precipitates include
Ni3Ti, Ni3Mo, laves phase, ω phase, σ phase, etc. [15–17]. The
morphology of precipitates includes rod, needle, flake, granular, etc.
[18,19]. The diversity of alloy compositions and heat treatment pro­
cesses gives rise to the complexity and variability of precipitates and
morphology. Viswanathan et al. [16] demonstrated the formation of the
intermetallic phase Ni3(Ti,Mo) and Fe2Mo by heterogeneous nucleation
on dislocations. Niu et al. [14] investigated the Ni–Ti rich clusters

studies could not overcome the trade-off between the strength and
toughness of maraging steels [16,21–23].
The high density and low lattice mismatch of nanoscale precipitates
facilitate enhanced toughness and ductility of maraging steel [24]. The
high-density dispersed nano-precipitates not only pin and obstruct the
movement of dislocations, but also serve as a distinctive type of sus­
tainable dislocation source and multiplication [25,26]. Modifying the
microstructure and improving the mechanical properties of materials
through duplex aging processes is a widely applied strategy for titanium
alloys and aluminum alloys [27,28]. Nucleation on pre-existing surfaces
decreases the surface energy per unit volume and the interfacial energy
of nucleation [29]. Pre-aging increases the nucleation rate and decreases
the growth rate of the precipitates, resulting in finely dispersed pre­
cipitates [19]. Therefore, a well-designed precipitation path that allows
Mo-enriched particles to form preferentially and act as heterogeneous
nucleation sites for the later-formed precipitates would ultimately affect
the mechanical properties. However, due to the large atomic radius and
low diffusion coefficient of Mo, the precipitation pathway mentioned
above has not been achieved [30].
In this study, we aimed to regulate the formation sequence of pre­
cipitates through the duplex aging treatment (DAT) process. This is
implemented by controlling the heterogeneous nucleation sites to in­
crease the nucleation rate and reduce the size of the precipitates. As a
result, the maraging steel was processed by DAT routes to obtain
different microstructures and mechanical properties. In addition, the insitu synchrotron X-ray diffraction was used to investigate the variation
of dislocation density with strain in DAT steel. To summarize, maraging
steel with an excellent combination of ultra-high strength and fracture
toughness was obtained, which sheds light on the preparation of highperformance steel.

4. Discussion
4.1. Evolution of microstructure during heat treatment
The size of the precipitates in the DAT sample was smaller than that
in the SAT sample, and more importantly, the process of precipitate
formation was different in the DAT sample. The higher number density
and volume fraction of Ni3Ti precipitates were attributed to the het­
erogeneous nucleation sites provided by the Mo-enriched particles. The
enrichment of Mo elements was facilitated by the fact that cobalt re­
duces the solubility of Mo in maraging steel [47] and the low diffusion
rate of Mo in α-Fe [48]. The formation energy values indicate the pref­
erential segregation of solute atoms, which are 0.133 eV and 0.071 eV
for Ni and Mo in α-Fe, respectively [49]. As a result, Mo will precipitate
from the matrix during the subsequent cooling after SST (Fig. 2f). In the
SAT sample, acicular Ni3Ti precipitates preferentially form near the
dislocations and within the martensitic lath during aging at 480 ◦ C
(Fig. 3b). This is because the high energy near the dislocations lowers
the energy barrier for nucleation [50].
The aging treatment at 370 ◦ C increases the density of the Moenriched particles and prevents the formation of Ni3Ti precipitates
(Fig. 4a). The diffusion rate of atoms at 370 ◦ C is significantly low,
thereby impeding the occurrence of atomic transitions. The energy
provided cannot overcome the phase transformation energy barrier
necessary to form Ni3Ti [51]. The fine precipitates exhibit a tendency to
coarsen, thereby forming larger particles in order to minimize the
overall interfacial energy [52]. Therefore, when the SST sample was
aged at 370 ◦ C, the Mo-enriched particles grew into larger particles
(Fig. 4b). Nucleation attached to pre-existing surfaces can decrease the
interfacial energy of nucleation [29]. The Mo partitioning increases the
chemical driving force and reduces the strain energy for the nucleation
of the precipitates [20]. According to the heterogeneous nucleation
theory, the Mo-enriched particles are precursors to Ni3Ti and can act as
nucleation sites for Ni3Ti, lowering the nucleation barrier [53]. During
the aging process, the aging temperature provides thermodynamic
conditions [19]. Therefore, the Mo-enriched particles formed after

4.2. Strengthening mechanism of maraging steel after different aging
treatments
For an in-depth analysis of the deformation mechanism, we per­
formed geometric phase analysis on HRTEM images after different aging
treatments [61]. The movement of dislocations is obstructed by pre­
cipitates bypassing the particles via the Orowan bypass mechanism, as
detailed in Section 3.2. The bending of the dislocations increases line
tension and requires greater applied stress to allow the dislocations to
glide over the particles [62]. As deformation increases, it produces
significant precipitation strengthening and leaves bent dislocation
morphology within the matrix, as evidenced in Fig. 10a and d. Dislo­
cations bypass the nanoprecipitates, inducing rapid multiplication of
dislocations after deformation [25]. Fig. 10b and e displays the HRTEM
images along the [110]α axis, where the rectangular and elliptical boxes
indicate the positions of Ni3Ti and ω precipitates, respectively. The se­
vere distortion in the vicinity of the precipitates forms high localized
strain fields, indicating severe interactions between dislocations and
precipitates, as shown in Fig. 10c. Differently, due to the higher number
density of Ni3Ti and ω particles in the DAT sample, more localized strain
fields are generated during deformation, as depicted in Fig. 10f. At the
same time, the reduced size of Ni3Ti makes the dislocations more sus­
ceptible to short-range motions, thus bypassing the precipitate particles
[39]. The dislocation and precipitate interactions lead to the accumu­
lation of plastic strain in the vicinity of the precipitates, as illustrated in
Fig. 10f. This massive dislocation pile-up in the vicinity of the pre­
cipitates is responsible for the high strength of the DAT sample (Fig. 7a).

4.3. Toughening mechanism of maraging steel after different aging
treatments

despite its high yield strength.
Based on the multi-scale characterization results, the microstructure
evolution of maraging steel with different aging treatments is sche­
matically illustrated in Fig. 13. The dislocation density of DAT samples
decreased slightly compared with the SAT samples (Fig. 13a and d). In
the SAT sample, the acicular Ni3Ti precipitates are preferentially formed
near the dislocations and within the martensitic lath (Fig. 13b). This is
because the high energy near the dislocations lowers the energy barrier
for nucleation. As the aging time increases, some of the Mo elements are
segregated at the interface between Ni3Ti and the matrix (Fig. 13c).
However, many tiny spherical Mo-enriched particles form preferentially
near the dislocations in the DAT sample (Fig. 13e). The Mo-enriched
particles act as heterogeneous nucleation sites for Ni3Ti particles,
increasing the nucleation rate of Ni3Ti and resulting in fine and uniform
particle distribution [54]. As aging proceeds at 480 ◦ C, the acicular
Ni3Ti particles appear not only near the dislocations within the matrix
but also at the interface between the Mo-enriched particles and the
matrix (Fig. 13f). With the further prolongation of aging, the Mo element
aggregates and grows to form the ω particles [57]. This DAT increases
the density of the precipitates, leading to significant precipitation
strengthening. Meanwhile, the refinement of the precipitate decreases
the lattice mismatch between the precipitate and the matrix, thereby
increasing toughness. In addition, a small amount of reversed austenite
is formed due to the aggregation of austenite stabilizing elements and
longer aging times (Fig. 13f) [58,60]. The reversed austenite undergoes
a TWIP effect during deformation, relieving stress concentration at the
crack tip and enhancing the toughness of the DAT sample [79]. Conse­
quently, the DAT breaks the conventional precipitation path and forms
finely dispersed precipitates, thereby enhancing the strength and frac­
ture toughness of maraging steels [39].

One of the outstanding advantages of the DAT sample was the high
fracture toughness combined with considerable strength. The fracture
morphology after tensile testing revealed that the DAT samples exhibi­
ted a cup-shaped fracture with numerous dimples, which was typical of
ductile fracture. The dimples of the SAT samples were large and deep,
while those of the DAT samples were small and shallow (Fig. S3). To
further elucidate the toughness mechanism, the C(T) fracture toughness
samples were analyzed using EBSD and SEM. Fig. 12a shows that the
crack propagation path in the SAT sample exhibits an almost flat facet
with no secondary cracks (Fig. 12b). However, Fig. 12e displays a more
tortuous path of crack extension in the DAT sample, featuring many
secondary cracks (Fig. 12f). The primary crack extension is obstructed
by fine precipitates, leading to crack deflection and the formation of
multiple secondary cracks, thus increasing the crack nucleation energy
[76]. The EBSD band contrast image (Fig. 12c) indicates that the crack in
the SAT sample crosses the martensitic lath flat and is almost unob­
structed. The kernel average misorientation (KAM) image shows fewer
and smaller deformation areas, indicating lower toughness (Fig. 12d).
However, the crack through the martensitic laths was more curved and
generated multiple secondary cracks along the grain or lath boundaries
(Fig. 12g). The large and numerous stress concentration regions were
observed in the DAT sample, demonstrating greater deformation regions
and better toughness [77], as shown in Fig. 12h. A lower lattice
mismatch between the precipitates and the matrix, which in turn re­
duces the potential for cracks at the precipitates-matrix interface, in­
creases the crack nucleation energy [17]. More reversed austenite can be
generated owing to the duplex aging process with the aggregation of
austenite stabilizing elements and longer aging time (Fig. S2). The
transformation of reversed austenite to martensite provided additional
transformation-induced plasticity toughening, which can blunt the crack
tip and increase crack propagation energy [40,78]. Therefore, these
synergistic mechanisms contribute to good toughness in the DAT sample
